SafeGuard

I developed SafeGuard as a way to review AI integrations, document controls and supporting evidence, and write findings that a business can act on. The assessment method and example reports are available; supporting software is under development.

Background

Organizations evaluating AI tools are asked to make security and risk decisions from uneven evidence. Vendor claims, technical architecture, data handling practices, and actual controls can be hard to compare. A checklist by itself may produce an answer without making clear what was checked or why a finding matters.

I wanted an assessment method that could turn that uncertainty into a reviewable record: what the system does, which risks apply, which controls are expected, what evidence supports the conclusion, and what an organization should do next.

Approach

I developed the assessment methodology and illustrative reports before treating the software as the deliverable. The methodology is designed to be applied manually today; the platform under development is intended to make evidence handling and consistency checks more repeatable.

Establish scope

Identify the AI use case, data touched, integration boundaries, intended users, and meaningful business consequences.

Evaluate controls

Use NIST AI RMF, its Generative AI Profile, and NIST CSF 2.0 as reference frameworks for evaluating security and governance controls. Framework alignment is not the same as proof of compliance.

Preserve evidence

Connect findings to the observations or documents that support them, and distinguish known facts from assumptions or missing information.

Recommend action

Prioritize findings by severity, describe business impact, and propose feasible mitigations with human review of conclusions.

My contribution: I established the assessment structure, risk reporting approach, and example deliverables, and I am directing development of software to support evidence management, control-coverage review, consistency checks, and report safeguards.

Results and evidence

I published three illustrative assessments for fictional accounting, healthcare, and energy organizations through Kingfisher Advisory. They show the assessment format and how findings connect to evidence and recommendations.

These are examples of the method, not client engagements or completed production assessments.

Limitations

  • The sample assessments are illustrative; they do not demonstrate client adoption, operational effectiveness, or independent audit certification.
  • Assessment quality still depends on the completeness and trustworthiness of supplied evidence.
  • Framework mappings are interpretation aids, not assurances that a system is legally compliant or risk-free.
  • Software functions should be represented as implemented only after they are tested and validated.

What I learned

Designing the reports helped me work through how to scope an AI use case, identify gaps in evidence, and make recommendations specific enough for someone to act on.

My enterprise consulting experience shaped the approach: an assessment is more useful when each finding has a clear owner, an actionable recommendation, and a way to verify the change.
